In 1966, a British survey ship found oil off the coast of a small trading port called Dubai.
It should have been the beginning of another petrostate. Instead, it became a warning the ruling family took more seriously than anyone expected.
Because the oil wasn't much. A fraction of what sat under Abu Dhabi, just down the coast. And Sheikh Rashid bin Saeed Al Maktoum did the math early: this well would run dry in a generation. Maybe two.
So while other Gulf states built economies on the assumption that the oil would last forever... Dubai built one on the assumption that it wouldn't.
Every dollar of crude revenue got funneled somewhere else. A deep-water port -- Jebel Ali -- dredged by hand in the 1970s, built for a shipping volume that didn't exist yet. An airport expanded years before the demand justified it. Free trade zones where foreign companies could own 100% of their business -- unheard of anywhere else in the region.
It looked, to a lot of people at the time, like a rounding error. A vanity project funded by a resource that was already running out.
By the 1990s, oil was under 20% of Dubai's economy. Today, it's less than 1%.
What replaced it wasn't found in the ground. It was built on top of it -- Emirates Airlines, connecting anywhere to anywhere through a single hub. The Dubai International Financial Centre, a legal island inside the country with its own courts, its own regulations, built specifically to make Western banks comfortable parking money there. And the skyline -- the Burj Khalifa, the Palm -- less an accident of architecture than a two-decade advertisement, telling the world this was a place capital could feel safe.
None of it was inevitable. Half of it should have failed. Jebel Ali sat nearly empty for years. The 2009 debt crisis nearly took the whole model down with it, and only a bailout from Abu Dhabi -- the same neighbor with all the oil Dubai never had -- kept it standing.
But the bet paid off. Dubai turned a resource it was about to lose into the one thing that outlasts resources: infrastructure other people need.
The oil wells that started it all still pump, quietly, a footnote now. The city built to survive without them is the one anybody's heard of.
